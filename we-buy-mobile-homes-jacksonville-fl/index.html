Why a Mobile Home Won't Sell Like a House

Three things stop most mobile home sales in Duval, Clay, Nassau, and Putnam counties.

Financing

Almost no buyer can get a loan

Most lenders won't write a mortgage on a manufactured home — especially one built before June 15, 1976, which has no HUD tag and doesn't qualify at all. That knocks out nearly every retail buyer and leaves you waiting on someone with cash.

Title

It's a title, not a deed

If the home was never retired to real property, the state treats it like a vehicle: it has a DMV title, not a deed. A lost title, a dead owner still on the title, or a lien the seller forgot about will stop a closing cold. We deal with these constantly.

The park

The park has to approve your buyer

On a rented lot, the park manager has final say over who moves in — and back lot rent usually has to be cleared at closing. A retail buyer walks when they learn this. We already know how the approval and lot-rent payoff work.

What Actually Sets the Price on a Jacksonville Mobile Home

Five things move the number more than square footage does.

1. Land-owned or park lot

This is the single biggest factor. A doublewide on its own acre is real estate and prices like real estate. The same home on a rented lot is personal property with a monthly bill attached, and it is worth a fraction of the land-owned version. Tell us which one you have and the number changes immediately.

2. Year built and the HUD tag

June 15, 1976 is the dividing line. Homes built after it carry a HUD certification label and can be moved, financed by specialty lenders, and insured normally. Homes built before it usually cannot — which does not make them unsellable, it just means the buyer pool is cash buyers like us.

3. Whether it was ever tied to the land

If a prior owner filed to retire the title and convert the home to real property, it transfers by deed with the land. If not, it transfers by title through the tax collector's office. Both are workable. Not knowing which one you have is what delays closings.

4. Roof, floors, and water

We buy as-is and we don't ask you to fix anything, but soft floors, an active roof leak, or a well shared with a neighbor all show up in the number. We'd rather tell you what we saw than quote high and re-trade you later.

5. Who's living in it

A tenant behind on rent, a family member who won't leave, or a relative with a life estate does not kill the deal. It changes the structure. On one Duval County closing we wrote a plan around a senior heir's life estate and her shared well specifically so she kept her water and her living situation.

Cash or Monthly Terms — You See Both Numbers

The same home is worth two different prices depending on how you get paid.

The cash number

One price, as-is, we typically cover standard closing costs, and you pick the date. This is the fastest exit and it is the lower of the two numbers. Choose it when the home is a headache you want gone.

The terms number

We pay a higher total price in exchange for paying you over time, monthly, secured and closed by an attorney. Choose it when you don't need all the money at once and you'd rather have income. On mobile homes the gap between cash and terms is often wider than it is on houses, because the cash market for them is so thin.

The truth check

We also show you what you'd realistically net listing it — including what a real estate commission and repairs would cost you on a home that most buyers can't finance. If listing nets more, we say so.
